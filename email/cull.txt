Dear Dr. Cull,

I hope this email finds you well. I am a transgender (non-binary) doctoral student of biology who believes gender abolitionism. I am writing to raise some questions I have after reading your paper Against Abolition and book What Gender Should Be. They're very interesting and insightful works. However, I have some questions that I cannot figure out. Could you kindly help me to answer them?

The first question is about your argument against gender abolitionism in the paper. You argued that abolishing gender means not recognising gender identity, which many transgender people rely on. However, if I'm not mistaken, you assumed that transgender status is something independent of history and culture, and will persist after gender construct being abolished. However, you did not talk about the ontological status of gender identity and how could it survive gender abolition. You wrote a chapter "Gender identity, deflated" but why would gender identity persist after gender abolition? I think the "gender phenomenology" is still a product of internalising gender stereotypes, therefore it cannot survive in a post-gender world. 
Gender identity is defined as one's feeling of their gender. If it is a mental state concerning "gender", then it must have certain contents of gender. If in the new world, people have never experienced gender socialisation and never developed a so-called "gender identity" from the beginning, then they have nothing to lose. 

Secondly, I think "logically invalidate" an identity is not necessarily harmful to the specific people. For example, many communist workers consider that we will be freer in a communist world where identities like capitalist and worker no longer exist. Many transgender people find that, not only their specific gender identity, but also the general term "transgender" is also meaningful. However, if we now completely stop the birth sex assignment for new infants, they will not be able to be cis- or transgender. Because the opposition of "cis/transgender" is defined by the agreement between one's assigned sex and gender identity. If our "assigned sexes" does not exist at the beginning, then we cannot compare the two things, therefore nobody will be cis- or transgender. This is obviously liberating rather than oppressive to us. This shows that "logically invalidating an identity" is not a sufficient condition of "harming the corresponding people."

Thirdly, you said that "we can abolish gender stereotypes and protect gender identity" in What Gender Should Be. However (same as above), how could there be "gender identity" if there are no gender stereotypes? And in the same book, you said that in the future, gender might be understood as an aesthetic style (pp. 169). While aren't masculine or feminine aesthetic styles also a specific kind of gender stereotypes? But you said that gender stereotypes should be abolished. If masculine or feminine aesthetic styles should be retained, it means that not all gender stereotypes should be abolished. This is self-contradictory. 

Lastly, I have serious doubts about "gender pluralism" (if I'm not mistaken, it means creating new gender labels to include more experience). Because "gender" is a very omni-encompassing concept, it includes many different, ontologically unrelated things. Their combination can create an exponential explosion. I don't think it's mathematically feasible to create enough labels. Even if we do create them, it will definitely create a cognitive inequality between them. Because we cannot remember so many labels. 

I would greatly appreciate your feedback, if you are willing to provide!

Best wishes,
